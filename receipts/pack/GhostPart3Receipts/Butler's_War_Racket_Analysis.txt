### **The Powder, Steel, and Heavy Industry Syndicates**

The munitions and heavy infrastructure conglomerates experienced near-instantaneous financial mutations, transforming stable pre-war enterprises into unprecedented engines of wealth.

* **The Du Ponts:** The "powder people," who provided the foundational explosives required for the slaughter, saw their average yearly profit jump from $6,000,000 in the pre-war period (1910-1914) to $58,000,000 during the war years (1914-1918)—representing a staggering 950% increase.4  
* **Bethlehem Steel:** Shunting aside the manufacture of civilian rails, girders, and bridges to pivot entirely to the production of lucrative war materials, their average yearly profits surged from $6,000,000 to $49,000,000.4  
* **United States Steel:** The largest of the steel syndicates saw profits vault from a robust pre-war average of $105,000,000 per year to an astronomical $240,000,000 per year during the conflict.21  
* **International Nickel Company:** As Butler noted, "you can't have a war without nickel." Recognizing their indispensable position, the company showed an increase from a mere average of $4,000,000 a year to $73,500,000 yearly—an undeniable, exponential leap of more than 1,700 percent.2

### **The Copper Syndicates**

The copper industry, always a primary beneficiary of prolonged kinetic warfare due to its utility in munitions and communications, leveraged the global slaughter into extreme capital consolidation.

* **Anaconda Copper:** Average yearly earnings during the pre-war years (1910-1914) sat at a respectable $10,000,000. During the war years, those profits leaped violently to $34,000,000 per year.2  
* **Utah Copper:** A pre-war average of $5,000,000 per year jumped dramatically to a wartime average of $21,000,000 per year.2  
* **The Copper Cohort:** Grouping these major copper giants with three smaller entities reveals a total yearly average pre-war profit of $137,480,000 for the sector. Then along came the war, and this group's average yearly profits skyrocketed to $408,300,000—a nearly 200 percent increase across the entire cohort.2

The extraction of public wealth was not limited to heavy munitions and raw metals; the basic logistical requirements of outfitting, feeding, and maintaining a massive conscripted force provided equally lucrative, highly exploitable chokepoints.

* **Central Leather Company:** For the three-year period preceding the war, total profits were $3,500,000 (equating to approximately $1,167,000 annually). In 1916 alone, leveraging the demand for boots, saddles, and harnesses, Central Leather returned a staggering profit of $15,500,000—an increase of 1,100 percent.2  
* **General Chemical Company:** Averaging a profit of a little over $800,000 a year before the war, their profits jumped to $12,000,000 upon the commencement of hostilities, representing a leap of 1,400 percent.2  
* **American Sugar Refining Company:** Averaging a modest $200,000 a year for the three years prior to the war, a profit of $6,000,000 was recorded in 1916\.2  
* **The Shoe Syndicates:** Utilizing the conflict to offload inventory at premium prices, shoe manufacturers sold the United States government 35,000,000 pairs of hobnailed service shoes for an army of only 4,000,000 soldiers. This averaged over eight pairs per soldier, generating abnormal profits, despite the grim reality that frontline regiments—including Butler's own—were frequently left with only a single pair of boots.2  
* **Textile Profiteering:** Basic undershirts costing a mere 14 cents to manufacture were sold to the government for 30 to 40 cents each, ensuring a handsome, guaranteed profit margin for textile syndicates completely insulated from market risk.25

The most damning statistical receipt regarding the sheer breadth and normalization of the racket is contained within Senate Document No. 259, generated by the Sixty-Fifth Congress reporting on corporate earnings and government revenues.8 This document forensically audited the wartime profits of 122 meat packers, 153 cotton manufacturers, 299 garment makers, 49 steel plants, and 340 coal producers.8
